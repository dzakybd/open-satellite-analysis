## A Satellite Image Full of Smoke

On 21 October 2026, Sentinel-2 passed over Pulang Pisau in Central Kalimantan, in a week when NASA's VIIRS sensors logged about 1,400 fire hotspots inside a single 14 km square there. In true colour, the picture shows nothing but smoke.

The same pass also recorded shortwave infrared, and in that band the smoke mostly disappears. What shows instead is a burn scar several kilometres long and the line of the fire front at its edge.

![Pulang Pisau, 21 Oct 2026: smoke in true colour, the burn scar and fire front in shortwave infrared, and the burn-severity map with NASA VIIRS hotspots on top.](/media/open-satellite-analysis/pulangpisau-hero.png)

That image is free. So is everything needed to turn it into a number: the imagery, the index formulas, and NASA's fire detections. What is missing is the glue, the steps between "here is a scene" and "about 2,900 hectares burned, and here is the evidence". Those are the steps where people, and AI agents, go wrong. I built [open-satellite-analysis](https://github.com/dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis) to do them.

**Code:** [github.com/dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis](https://github.com/dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis) (MIT) · **Demo:** [huggingface.co/spaces/dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis](https://huggingface.co/spaces/dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis)

> **TL;DR**
>
> - **What it is:** a skill that answers questions about a place from Sentinel-2. Give it an address or coordinates and a question; it returns numbers, maps and the limits that apply. Two versions, like [pid-parser](/en/blog/pid-parser-pid-to-graph): a skill for coding agents (`npx skills add dzakybd/open-satellite-analysis`) and a LangChain Deep Agents version.
> - **Free data, no keys:** Sentinel-2 from Earth Search (Element 84, on AWS), Landsat 8/9 from Microsoft Planetary Computer, 245 indices from the Awesome Spectral Indices catalog computed with spyndex, NASA FIRMS VIIRS and MODIS hotspots, and OpenStreetMap geocoding.
> - **On the Pulang Pisau fires:** Sentinel-2 found about 2,925 ha of confident burn (dNBR ≥ 0.27) by 21 October, in the third of a 196 km² area it could see through the smoke; 266 of 318 visible VIIRS hotspots (83.6%) land within 190 m of it. Landsat 8 saw the whole area on 4 October: about 4,000 ha.
> - **Where it is weak:** smoke hides the ground for weeks at the peak of the fires, the severity classes come from temperate forests, and 20 m pixels miss small fires.

## What It Is Useful For

The same steps answer more than fire questions. Each needs only an area, one or two dates, and the right index:

- **Forest and land fires (karhutla):** how many hectares burned, how severely, and whether the burn map agrees with the hotspots.
- **Clearing after a cutoff date:** did this plot lose vegetation after 31 December 2020, the EU deforestation regulation's cutoff?
- **Floods:** how much of an area is water now compared with a normal month (NDWI).
- **Mining and earthworks:** how far bare soil has spread around a pit (bare-soil indices), and whether it crosses a boundary.
- **Crops and plantations:** whether vegetation in a field dropped in a season, for example to check a drought claim (NDVI, EVI).

I tested the fire workflow end to end. The others use the same scripts with a different index, so treat them as what the output supports, not as measured results.

## How It Works, Step by Step

**1. Pin the place.** `locate.py` turns an address into coordinates with OpenStreetMap's Nominatim, or takes coordinates directly, and draws a square area of interest (AOI) around them. Here: 7 km either side of -2.8, 114.05, which Nominatim places in Pulang Pisau regency.

**2. Pick the index.** A spectral index combines bands to highlight one thing. NBR, the Normalized Burn Ratio, uses near-infrared, which healthy leaves reflect strongly, and shortwave infrared, which bare and charred ground reflects strongly: `(NIR − SWIR2) / (NIR + SWIR2)`. When vegetation burns, NBR drops.

I did not write any formulas. The [Awesome Spectral Indices](https://github.com/awesome-spectral-indices/awesome-spectral-indices) catalog lists 280 indices with their formulas, bands, application domain and source paper, and [spyndex](https://github.com/awesome-spectral-indices/spyndex) computes them. 245 work on Sentinel-2; the rest need a band it does not have, such as thermal. `search_index.py --domain burn` lists the ten burn indices Sentinel-2 can compute.

**Why the catalog's band names need care.** The catalog names bands generically: `N` is near-infrared, `S1` and `S2` are the two shortwave bands. So in NBR's formula `S2` means SWIR 2 (band B12), not Sentinel-2. The skill spells that out, because an agent will confuse them.

**3. Find a clear scene, for your area.** Sentinel-2 images the same place about every five days in tiles of 110 km × 110 km, and each tile comes with a cloud percentage. That number is for the whole tile. `find_scene.py` instead reads the scene classification layer, a per-pixel mask of cloud, shadow, water and ground, for the AOI only.

It matters. The 21 October scene is listed at 13.6% cloud. Over our AOI, 66% was smoke or cloud, because the smoke sits exactly where the fires are.

**4. Compute.** `compute_index.py` reads only the pixels of the AOI from the cloud-optimized files on AWS, puts every band on one 20 m grid, converts to surface reflectance, and computes NBR and NDVI. It also saves two pictures: true colour, and a SWIR false colour (B12, B8, B4), where burn scars are dark red, healthy vegetation green, and fire fronts bright orange.

**Why the reflectance step nearly broke everything.** Since 2022, ESA adds 1000 to every Sentinel-2 L2A value, and the metadata says to subtract it. Earth Search already removes it from the pixels and marks the item `earthsearch:boa_offset_applied`, but the metadata still lists the offset. My first run applied it again: shortwave values went negative and healthy forest came out at NDVI −0.99. A second bug made it worse: the `visual` asset, an 8-bit RGB picture, also claims to be bands 2, 3 and 4, and was read as red. Both are fixed and written into the skill, so an agent does not repeat them.

**5. Compare the two dates.** `compare.py --burn` subtracts the "after" NBR from the "before" NBR. The difference, dNBR, is a standard measure of burn severity, and the USGS FIREMON ranges (Key & Benson, 2006) turn it into classes from unburned to high severity.

The script reports two areas. **Confident burn** is dNBR ≥ 0.27, moderate severity and up. **Possible burn** is the low-severity class, 0.1 to 0.27, which haze, drought and harvest can also produce. On my first site, a hazy scene put 3,600 ha into "low severity", and the true-colour image showed it was mostly haze.

**6. Check against hotspots.** NASA FIRMS publishes VIIRS fire detections, 375 m pixels from several passes a day, free for the last seven days. `hotspots.py` pulls the ones inside the AOI, and `compare.py --hotspots` checks how many fall within 190 m of a confident burn pixel. It is an independent check: a different satellite, a different sensor, a different method.

## The Result for Pulang Pisau

| | |
|---|---|
| Area | 14 km × 14 km around -2.8, 114.05 (19,602 ha) |
| Before | 29 August 2026, clear (0% of the AOI unusable) |
| After | 21 October 2026, 66% of the AOI under smoke or cloud |
| Visible on both dates | 33.9% of the AOI |
| Confident burn (dNBR ≥ 0.27) | ~2,925 ha |
| Possible burn (low severity) | ~1,508 ha |
| VIIRS hotspots, 17–21 Oct, on visible pixels | 318, of which 266 (83.6%) on a confident burn |

The hectares cover only the third the satellite could see, so the true burned area is larger. The agreement with the hotspots is the part I trust most: two independent measurements point at the same ground. (The hotspot files were the Suomi NPP and NOAA-20 ones, downloaded on 24 October.)

## A Second Satellite Through the Gap

Sentinel-2 is not the only free optical satellite. Landsat 8 and 9 image the same ground every eight days between them, on different days, at 30 m. On Earth Search their files sit in a requester-pays bucket that needs AWS credentials, but Microsoft Planetary Computer serves them with a token its API hands out anonymously, so `--source landsat` needs no account either.

For Pulang Pisau that filled the gap. Landsat 8 passed on 4 October and its cloud mask flagged none of the area. True colour shows a smoke plume; shortwave infrared shows the burn scar, with the fire front glowing along its southern edge.

![Landsat 8 on 4 October 2026 over the same area: a smoke plume in true colour, the burn scar and a glowing fire front in shortwave infrared, and the dNBR map against 25 August.](/media/open-satellite-analysis/pulangpisau-landsat.png)

| Landsat 8/9 | |
|---|---|
| Before / after | 25 Aug 2026 (Landsat 9) / 4 Oct 2026 (Landsat 8), 30 m |
| Visible on both dates | 100% of the AOI |
| Confident burn (dNBR ≥ 0.27) | ~4,003 ha |
| Possible burn (low severity) | ~2,561 ha |

The two numbers measure different things. Landsat sees the whole area up to 4 October; Sentinel-2 sees a third of it up to 21 October. The plume is thin enough that Landsat's mask let it through, so pixels under it carry some smoke, which SWIR is the least affected by.

**Which source for which question.** The skill gives the agent a short table: Sentinel-2 by default; Landsat when Sentinel-2 is hidden or for history; FIRMS for "is it burning now"; and two free sources it does not script yet but can point to, Sentinel-1 radar, which sees through smoke and cloud, and the Copernicus 30 m elevation model for low-lying, flood-prone ground.

## Asking a Deep Agent

The scripts are the same whether a person runs them or an agent does. I gave the LangChain Deep Agents version a plain question, with only the skill to go on:

> How much land burned this fire season within about 7 km of -2.8, 114.05 in Pulang Pisau, Central Kalimantan, since the end of August 2026? Check the burn map against this week's fire hotspots.

**First run: an honest answer to the wrong question.** The agent (gpt-6-luna, low reasoning effort) returned 177 ha, with nearly the whole area visible. It had searched forward from 31 August for the first clear scene and found 11 September, weeks before the October fires. To its credit, it said the hotspots fell outside its comparison dates. The skill had said "take the latest scene that shows the area", which an agent can read either way.

**The fix was a few lines of instructions and one line of output.** The skill now says: search the "after" image back from today with a looser cloud limit, never forward from the start date. And `find_scene.py` now prints the newer scenes it skipped for cloud, so the trade-off is on screen.

**Second run, same question:**

> About 2,925 ha of confident burn signal from 29 Aug to 21 Oct 2026, plus 1,508 ha of lower-severity change that is less certain. Only 33.9% of the area was visible on both dates, so these figures apply to the comparable portion, not the full area. Of 504 hotspots between the scene dates on jointly visible pixels, 424 (84.1%) were within 190 m of a confident-burn pixel.

That matches the run by hand to the hectare, and its hotspot check used all three VIIRS satellites. It took 272 seconds, 7 tool calls and about 47K input tokens.

The lesson is the same one as in pid-parser: what makes the agent reliable is not a bigger model but scripts that do the measuring and instructions that leave no room for the wrong reading.

## What the Smoke Hides

About 50 km to the northeast, around the densest hotspot cell of the week (2,226 detections in seven days), the same approach mostly fails, and that is worth showing too. From mid-September to 24 October, every Sentinel-2 scene of that tile reports 96–100% cloud, except one hazy pass on 1 October. The fires produce their own blanket.

That is the honest shape of satellite fire monitoring: the heat sensors see the fire as it burns, and the optical imagery measures the scar once the smoke lifts. A tool that pretends otherwise gives a confident wrong number.

## Limits and What I Would Improve Next

- **Smoke.** At the peak of the fires the ground can stay hidden for weeks. Landsat on other days helps; Sentinel-1 radar, which sees through smoke and cloud, is the next source to script.
- **Severity classes.** FIREMON was calibrated on temperate forests. On tropical peat, where fire burns down into the soil, the classes are relative severity, not a measurement of depth.
- **Resolution.** 20 m pixels miss fires under about half a hectare.
- **Hotspot history.** The free FIRMS files cover only the last seven days; older hotspots need a (free) key.
- **Attribution.** An index change shows that the surface changed, not why or who caused it. The skill says so in every answer.
